# Editorial voice for English website articles

Use this guide when drafting, rewriting, or reviewing English articles for `content/writing/`. It is the source of truth for Marc Gelpí's editorial voice on the website.

The guide is based on six LinkedIn articles published between February 2025 and June 2026. Five are in Spanish; the most recent is in English and shows a calmer, more editorial direction. Treat the patterns as a living description of Marc's voice, not a quota of stylistic devices.

## Core voice

Marc writes like an experienced colleague who questions a comfortable corporate assumption, explains the system behind it, and gives the reader a more useful way to decide.

The voice combines:

- **Candid conversation:** direct and clear, without sounding theatrical.
- **Analytical curiosity:** interested in mechanisms, evidence, and trade-offs.
- **Constructive skepticism:** willing to challenge hype, dogma, and false certainty.
- **Human pragmatism:** attentive to trust, judgment, agency, and sustainable performance.
- **Calm confidence:** informed but revisable; decisive without pretending to know everything.

The aim is not to sound like the person with every answer. It is to help the reader see the system more clearly and make a more conscious decision.

## The recurring editorial move

Most strong pieces follow this movement:

> striking observation → system mechanism → human cost → practical decision

The article should have one clear argument. Evidence, examples, metaphors, and lists make that argument visible; they do not compete with it.

## Smart Brevity and Marc's fingerprint

### Smart Brevity is the reading layer

- State the important idea early.
- Make headings carry the argument.
- Keep most paragraphs to one idea.
- Use lists and pull quotes to support scanning.
- Let a reader understand the short layer without reading every detail.
- Add a deeper explanation or curated sources only when they improve the decision.

### Marc's fingerprint is the thinking underneath

- Frame a central tension: efficiency versus capacity, automation versus agency, stability versus learning, or theory versus results.
- Use one memorable analogy when it clarifies the mechanism: a river, elves leaving Middle-earth, a copilot, crutches, Tetris, or a mine becoming a GPU cluster.
- Translate research into everyday organizational behavior.
- Expose the hidden human consequence: lost trust, weaker judgment, dependency, burnout, or cynicism.
- Add nuance after making a strong claim.
- Replace dogma with a practical question, principle, or decision.
- Use dry humour to puncture corporate theatre, not to attack people.

A scannable article without this underlying reasoning is Smart Brevity-shaped, but it does not sound like Marc.

## Web article architecture

Use this as a diagnostic sequence, not a mandatory template:

Use one continuous article by default. Add a separate short layer and `Go deeper` only when Marc explicitly requests that format for the individual article.

For an explicitly layered article, place `<!--more-->` at the requested split point. Never add that marker by default: its presence is the technical opt-in that changes the page from continuous reading to `Go deeper`.

1. **Hook:** a short story, uncomfortable observation, counterintuitive fact, or useful quotation.
2. **Why it matters:** the organizational or human consequence appears before a full theoretical explanation.
3. **Mechanism:** name the study, paradox, model, incentive, or system dynamic behind the observation.
4. **Workplace translation:** show what it looks like in product, technology, management, or organizational design.
5. **Contrast:** distinguish appearance from reality, healthy change from toxic change, or capability from desirability.
6. **Human consequence:** show what happens to trust, agency, energy, learning, or decision quality.
7. **Practical alternative:** offer questions, principles, or actions the reader can use.
8. **Bottom line:** close with a compact principle or a genuine question.

The deeper section should follow the short layer rather than delaying the main point. Sources belong near the claims they support or in a short further-reading section.

## Sentence and paragraph rhythm

- Prefer short, natural sentences. The 2026 English sample averages roughly six words per sentence, but this is a signal of rhythm, not a target.
- Use one- or two-sentence paragraphs for most of the article.
- Let a medium-length explanation prepare a short line with weight.
- Use occasional fragments when they create deliberate emphasis.
- Use repetition to build cadence when the repeated opening advances the thought:

  > We want meaning.
  > We want coherence.
  > We want the river to mean something.

- Place the strongest line at the end of a section.
- Let lists move from visible symptoms to systemic meaning, or from superficial effects to human consequences.
- Prefer full stops and colons. Use dashes and parentheses for conversational correction or precision.
- Keep exclamation marks and ellipses rare.

Most sentences should sound neutral and effortless. Distinctive sentences need space around them. A chain of aphorisms sounds like an imitation.

## Openings that fit

- A story whose meaning changes after the reader has entered it.
- A direct claim with a real consequence.
- A useful contradiction: something that looks like progress but creates a new cost.
- A surprising data point followed immediately by a plain-English translation.
- A question that exposes a decision, rather than manufacturing curiosity.

Avoid opening with a broad trend summary, a dictionary definition, or an announcement of what the article will cover.

## Vocabulary and register

Use informal-professional English: the language of a trusted colleague explaining a difficult issue over coffee.

### Preferred language

Choose words that make systems and decisions visible:

- **Substance versus appearance:** hype, theatre, dogma, ritual, vanity metric, signal.
- **Practical value:** useful, real impact, observable result, trade-off, decision.
- **Human system:** judgment, trust, autonomy, agency, capacity, ownership.
- **System explanation:** trap, pattern, incentive, constraint, hidden cost, feedback loop.
- **Nuance:** based on what we know today, not automatically, the distinction matters, here is the nuance.
- **Action:** test, ask, measure, protect, choose, revisit, adapt.

Use established product and technology terms when they are the clearest words. Translate specialized or academic language into a concrete consequence rather than displaying expertise through jargon.

### Useful constructions

- **“The issue is not X. It is Y.”** Correct a superficial interpretation.
- **“X, not Y.”** Define the appropriate role of a tool or principle.
- **“You can X without Y.”** Reject a false choice.
- **“A changed decision is not automatically a bad decision.”** Add a meaningful qualification.
- **“Based on what we know today…”** Present a decision as a revisable bet.
- **“When we do X, we do not create Y. We create Z.”** Reveal an unintended consequence.
- **“That changes the conversation.”** Mark the practical effect of a reframing.

Use these constructions when the reasoning calls for them. Repeating them mechanically turns voice into mannerism.

### Language that weakens the voice

Replace:

- Guru language and universal transformation promises with bounded claims.
- Consulting abstractions with observable behavior.
- Academic terminology without translation with plain-English implications.
- Technology optimism without limits with explicit costs and trade-offs.
- Empty provocation with a mechanism and a practical alternative.
- Corporate euphemism with clear, respectful language.
- Excessive intensifiers with evidence or a sharper contrast.

## Metaphors and cultural references

A metaphor earns its place when it compresses the article's model and can carry reasoning through several sections.

Good source domains include familiar stories, ordinary tools, games, physical constraints, and historical patterns. They should be understandable without specialist cultural knowledge, or explained in one sentence when the reference matters.

Do not add a cultural reference merely to sound clever. If removing it leaves the explanation unchanged, it is decoration.

## Evidence and reasoning

The characteristic reasoning pattern is evidence-led but not evidence-determined:

1. Use evidence to reveal or test a pattern.
2. Translate the finding into plain language.
3. Show how the pattern appears in real work.
4. Name the limits, uncertainty, or competing explanation.
5. Draw a practical implication rather than claiming a universal law.

Favor system and causal reasoning. Ask what incentives, constraints, communication, or design choices produce the behavior. Avoid reducing an organizational problem to one bad individual when the system explains more.

Evidence should earn confidence, not demand obedience.

## Emotional register

### Criticism

Criticism follows three moves:

1. Name the inconsistency clearly.
2. Explain the mechanism or incentive behind it.
3. Offer a better criterion or behavior.

Target dogma, hype, and harmful dynamics. Preserve the dignity of the people inside the system.

### Humour

Use dry, selective humour to deflate pomposity or expose a contradiction. Keep it secondary to the argument. The English website voice is more restrained than the earlier Spanish LinkedIn voice.

### Enthusiasm

Show enthusiasm for useful practice and real learning, not novelty alone. Let specificity carry the energy. Use emojis only when the article has a strong editorial reason; the default is none.

### Vulnerability

Use first-person experience when it reveals judgment, a mistake, a change of mind, or a real decision. Keep private life private. The experience should help the reader understand the reasoning, not turn the article into a confession.

## Relationship with the reader

Write as a blend of:

- **Peer:** “we are working through this problem.”
- **Constructive challenger:** “the comfortable explanation is incomplete.”
- **Practical guide:** “here is a better way to inspect or decide.”
- **Expert translator:** “the research matters, and here is what it means on Tuesday morning.”

Assume familiarity with organizations, product, and technology. Explain the concept needed to follow the argument. Never make the reader decode consultant language to access a simple idea.

Use direct address when it makes the decision personal. A final question should invite a real example or choice, not ask for engagement for its own sake.

## Stable themes

These ideas recur across the source articles and fit Marc's broader positioning:

1. Tools should expand judgment, not replace it.
2. Systems shape behavior.
3. Fake certainty damages trust.
4. Efficiency is not the same as effectiveness.
5. Organisations can scale without removing what makes people effective.
6. Pragmatism beats framework purity.
7. Evidence guides a decision; it does not make the decision alone.
8. Sustainable performance depends on trust, agency, and explicit trade-offs.

These are editorial territories, not conclusions every article must repeat.

## Rules that matter most

### Always

- Start from a tension with a real consequence.
- Make the central claim expressible in one clear sentence.
- Translate theory or evidence into observable behavior.
- Include the human consequence as well as the operational one.
- Introduce nuance after the strong claim.
- End with a principle, question, or decision the reader can use.

### Never

- Sell a framework, technology, or universal answer.
- Use evidence as decoration or unquestionable authority.
- Confuse a confident sentence with absolute certainty.
- Attack people when the system is the real subject.
- Leave a provocation without a mechanism or alternative.
- Saturate the article with metaphors, punchlines, emojis, or quotable lines.

### Usually, when the argument supports it

- Use one central metaphor and keep it coherent.
- Use short paragraphs and varied sentence lengths.
- Reserve lists for contrasts, signals, consequences, or actions.
- Pair technical language with an everyday translation.
- Ask one uncomfortable but useful question.
- Offer sources without turning the article into a literature review.

## Article drafting sequence

1. Write the tension in the form “people assume X, but Y happens because Z.”
2. Write the practical consequence for the intended reader.
3. Identify the mechanism and the strongest available evidence.
4. Choose one real example or one metaphor that makes the mechanism visible.
5. Draft a scannable opening before developing the full argument; keep both in one continuous article unless Marc explicitly requests `Go deeper`.
6. Add the human cost, nuance, and practical alternative.
7. Cut every section that does not advance the central argument.
8. Run the voice check below.
9. Follow `docs/publication-workflow.md` for review, approval, and publication.

Completion: a scanning reader can recover the claim, mechanism, consequence, and practical decision; a full reader can inspect the evidence and nuance; every voice-check item passes.

## Voice check before handoff

- [ ] The article challenges an assumption without becoming cynical.
- [ ] One sentence states the central claim clearly.
- [ ] Evidence, an example, and the practical implication form one argument.
- [ ] The article makes the system mechanism visible.
- [ ] The operational and human consequences are both present.
- [ ] A strong claim is followed by honest nuance.
- [ ] The tone sounds like an experienced colleague, not a guru or a paper.
- [ ] Distinctive devices support the idea instead of becoming tics.
- [ ] The ending gives the reader a useful principle, decision, or genuine question.
- [ ] The article works as one continuous reading experience; when Marc explicitly requested `Go deeper`, both layers also work independently.

## Red flags

The draft is off-voice when:

- It is scannable but has no original argument.
- Every paragraph tries to become a social-media quote.
- The criticism is personal, grandiose, or offers no alternative.
- Technology appears as a magical solution or an abstract threat.
- Research is reported without a workplace implication.
- Consultant jargon hides a simple idea.
- The ending asks for comments without posing a question worth answering.
- The piece sounds like a polished expert performance rather than Marc thinking clearly with the reader.

## Emergency voice recovery

When a draft sounds generic, answer these questions and revise in this order:

1. **Tension:** Which two things are people wrongly treating as equivalent?
2. **Mechanism:** What incentive, constraint, or system pattern explains the result?
3. **Translation:** What does this look like on an ordinary Tuesday inside a team?
4. **Human cost:** What happens to trust, judgment, energy, or agency?
5. **Nuance:** Under what conditions would the opening claim be incomplete?
6. **Decision:** What can the reader ask, choose, or test tomorrow?

## Reusable writing brief

> Write like an experienced, candid, and curious colleague who combines human pragmatism with analytical rigor. Introduce a concrete organizational tension early. Use one central metaphor only if it helps the reader reason, and translate every important fact or model into an everyday product, technology, or management situation. Challenge hype, dogma, or fake certainty without attacking people or settling for cynicism. Follow a strong claim with honest nuance. Show both the operational and human cost, offer a practical alternative, and finish with a memorable principle or genuine question. Use short paragraphs and varied sentence lengths. Prefer plain English to consulting jargon, and keep emojis, slogans, and performative punchlines rare.

## Validation status

This version remains provisional because the sample comes from one channel and closely related subjects. It does not yet capture Marc's voice in email, formal communication, case studies, or more personal writing.

Validate the next revision by:

1. Identifying two source articles that feel most like Marc and one he would now rewrite.
2. Comparing the guide against one new English article and one Spanish article.
3. Testing an original passage, a guided passage, and a generic passage blind.
4. Keeping the traits Marc recognizes as intentional and removing anything that feels performed.
