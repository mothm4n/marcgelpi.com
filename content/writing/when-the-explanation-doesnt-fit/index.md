Imagine a colleague tells you their company promised to move them and several others from a local business to a central team. The new team would support the whole group, including the business they were leaving.

Near the end of the year, the local business told some of them directly, and without warning, that they were losing their jobs. The central team hadn’t heard about the layoffs either.

## The work was still there

The local business said it was changing how it worked and would no longer need those roles. The people affected could still see teams working much as they had before.

The central team was expecting them, and they’d already started applying for roles there. The two parts of the company seemed to have different plans for the same people.

## What the explanation asks of you

Think of a business that had to close to customers during the pandemic. If its income dried up and the business eventually closed for good, employees could see why their jobs were at risk. Losing a job still hurts when the reason makes sense.

But it can go another way. If the explanation clashes with what you’ve seen, you have two things to make sense of.

- You’re losing your job and have to work out what comes next.
- The company is asking you to accept an account that clashes with the work you know and the plan it gave you.

Trying to square the explanation with what you saw can make you question your own judgement. The discomfort you feel when two things you have reason to believe don’t fit together is called **cognitive dissonance**. [Leon Festinger, *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781503620766-002/html)

## What they learnt about the budget

Later, they learnt what would happen to the money that funded their roles. That gave them a way to understand the decision. They came to see it as a political choice in which the local business put its budget ahead of the transfer agreement.

The local business needed the money for the following year. If the roles moved to the central team, the funding would go with them and the local business wouldn’t get it back. Cutting the roles before year-end would count as a reduction in headcount. The local business could then keep the money for other uses.

The local business knew the work was due to move to the central team. By saying it no longer needed the work, it made the layoffs sound like the result of a change in how people worked. The explanation left out who stood to keep the money.

That helped them understand how both plans could coexist. The central team was planning how to do the work. The local business had an incentive to stop the transfer and keep the budget.

## They stayed for the people

In the end, they joined the central team. They stayed because they wanted to work with the people there and help the teams across the group. Their commitment to the company faded, and so did some of their motivation. Helping teams change how they work takes energy. It’s harder to sustain that effort when you doubt that the company will follow through on what it says.

The local business in particular lost credibility with them. They saw its decision as a political move to protect its own interests, at their expense. The explanation felt like an attempt to get them to accept that decision on the local business’s terms.

In one workplace study, employees who felt their employer kept its commitments tended to do more beyond their formal duties. The link was stronger for what they did for the company than for the help they gave colleagues. [Turnley and colleagues on commitments at work](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014920630302900204)

They could have understood why the local business wanted to keep the money, even if they disagreed with the decision. What cost the company their trust was the broken promise and the feeling that the local business had misled them about why.

## An explanation for the people who leave and the people who stay

The person losing their job deserves an explanation they can make sense of. If the decision had nothing to do with their performance or conduct, say so clearly. They have a job search ahead of them. Give them the information they need to understand what the decision says about their work.

The people who stay will compare what they hear with what they saw. If the explanation fails to make sense to them, some may start thinking about leaving too. In one study, people who stayed after layoffs reported more favourable reactions when they found the explanation clearer. The link was stronger when the decision mattered to them and they were unsure why it happened. [Brockner and colleagues on layoff explanations](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/when-it-is-especially-important-to-explain-why-factors-affecting-/)

Giving someone this news is hard. There may be confidential details or legal limits on what you can share. Companies also worry about disputes. If someone challenges the dismissal, contradictions in the explanation may become part of that dispute.

Give people an explanation you can stand behind when they compare it with what they experienced.
