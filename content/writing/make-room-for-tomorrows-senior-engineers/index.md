If we stop hiring juniors, where will the seniors come from?

**Why it matters:** Directing AI requires judgment about the problem and what counts as a useful result. Companies share responsibility for developing that judgment, even when the investment reduces short-term delivery.

In its [October 2026 internal employment study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic), Anthropic reports that juniors' share of technical hires fell from **27% to 9%**. Hiring managers also placed greater emphasis on autonomy and judgment in their assessments of technical candidates.

This describes changing proportions at one rapidly growing company, where technical hiring continued to grow in absolute terms. It does not establish the future of entry-level work across the profession.

## Give senior engineers time to teach

With a senior already overloaded, training becomes extra work on top. Under delivery pressure, it is easy to deprioritise. The junior hears: "You'll have to figure it out yourself."

Some juniors may find another route or someone willing to help. Others can remain stuck, increasingly frustrated by work the team expects them to do without the support to learn it.

In [their 2024 account of a workplace-proximity study](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2024/01/the-power-of-proximity-how-working-beside-colleagues-affects-training-and-productivity/), Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington and Amanda Pallais describe the cost of mentoring. At one company, engineers near teammates received more feedback, particularly juniors. Seniors, who provided most of the mentoring, also wrote fewer programs.

I would limit senior engineers' work in progress (WIP): the work I expect them to advance at the same time. Supporting a junior needs to count when setting delivery commitments.

The manager must choose which other work to delay or stop. Otherwise, we leave learning dependent on a senior's spare capacity and a junior's ability to manage alone.

## Directing AI requires experience with consequences

Moving from artist to art director captures part of the change. In that analogy, the director brings experience of making decisions and living with the results. That experience helps them recognise a problem and challenge what someone else produces.

Anthropic's [analysis of Claude Code sessions](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-expertise) links stronger knowledge of the task with more successful sessions. Much of the gain appears between novice and intermediate levels. It infers expertise and success from transcripts. It does not measure business outcomes or establish that seniority causes better results.

AI may increasingly carry out the attempts and tests. We still need to understand what those tests establish and which consequences they leave unexamined.

That makes explicit investment in learning important. People need opportunities to encounter a problem, work through failed attempts and understand why a decision turned out badly.

Without that experience, we risk accepting AI's choices because we cannot judge them. We remain responsible for deciding what we want to achieve and which trade-offs we accept, even when AI proposes the approach.

## Give juniors responsibility they can exercise

Protected time needs to support real decisions. I want juniors to practise choosing an approach and examining its consequences, with an experienced colleague available to help.

Consider a junior responsible for a small improvement to a user workflow. The junior and senior agree the scope, the checks needed and which decisions require review.

The junior might use AI to implement an approach and run the checks. I would ask them to explain what they expected, what happened and how that changes their next decision.

The senior helps them identify missed consequences and question their reasoning, while leaving them room to decide within the agreed scope. If the senior supplies every answer, the junior has less opportunity to practise deciding.

AI can let them try an idea sooner. They still need opportunities to face uncertainty and work through a failed attempt, with support when they get stuck. I want them to understand the outcome well enough to question the next proposal, including one the AI makes.

Their responsibility also includes understanding what should improve for the person using the product. That gives them a reason to question the proposed change itself, as well as how to build it.

## An urgent pause needs an end

A production incident may require the senior's full attention. I would accept postponing the planned learning to address an urgent problem.

I would also agree when to resume it and which commitments need to change.

If each new deadline cancels the time to support juniors, leaders need to address that recurring choice. The junior keeps waiting for opportunities to develop. The senior continues handling decisions the junior has had little chance to practise.

I would accept delivering less in the short term to give people that opportunity. We need to review whether they are developing greater judgment and independence.

At your next planning meeting, which delivery commitment will you reduce so a junior can develop the judgment to direct AI?
