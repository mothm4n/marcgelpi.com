Delegation requires someone willing to transfer power and someone willing and able to use it.

**Why it matters:** Announcing autonomy can leave a manager waiting for decisions and a team waiting for permission. Changing that arrangement requires an agreement both sides want to sustain, including when they disagree.

**An agreement between two sides:** Both sides have equal standing in the conversation, even with different roles and obligations. Whoever transfers power accepts that the other side will decide. Whoever accepts it takes responsibility for that decision.

Both sides need room to explain what they want to transfer or accept, and what they need to do so. A team can also ask the manager to retain a decision while it still needs support.

Management 3.0 [distinguishes authority from ability](https://management30.com/empower-teams/delegation-empowerment/). Giving permission and developing the ability to use it require different work. Information, skills and available time shape what we can accept. Delegation also leaves formal obligations attached to each role.

For example, a team wants to choose its onboarding experiments but needs to understand costs and commercial risk. It can agree to decide with the manager while obtaining the data and learning. Transferring more power requires preparing those conditions.

**Advice can hide a permission request:** I hear people say they own a decision, then ask someone else before acting. Sometimes they want perspective, alignment or an opinion from someone they admire. Sometimes they want security: acting feels safer with that person's approval.

The difference appears when they disagree. Within the agreed scope, can they choose something different from that person's recommendation?

[Management 3.0's delegation levels](https://management30.com/practice/delegation-poker/) distinguish who has the final say:

- At **Consult**, the person asking for opinions decides.
- At **Agree**, both sides decide together.
- At **Advise**, the person receiving advice decides.

If the supposed adviser retains the final decision, the actual arrangement resembles Consult. If both sides need to agree, it resembles Agree. Calling it Advise hides that difference.

Asking for advice is compatible with deciding. Waiting for approval changes who can decide.

**Past experience still matters:** If acting independently led to punishment, asking permission can become a way to protect yourself. Years of that pattern can make newly announced autonomy difficult to trust.

Research on [learned helplessness](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/learnedhelplessnessat50.pdf) examines the relationship between lack of control and passivity. At work, that concept helps us ask what someone expects to happen when they decide. Asking permission alone does not demonstrate helplessness.

Check their actual authority, the support they need and how the manager responds when they use it. To rebuild trust, start with a bounded decision and respect the agreement, including a choice you dislike. Review mistakes with the person and agree what to change.

**From current authority to desired authority:** [Delegation Poker](https://management30.com/practice/delegation-poker/) uses seven levels to make differences visible. The cards show where our expectations differ. We still need to have the conversation about power, willingness and ability ourselves.

In the exercise I use, we structure that conversation like this:

1. Define the two sides. Clarify who can transfer or accept power in that relationship.
2. Choose specific decision areas. Choosing an experiment and changing prices involve different decisions and boundaries.
3. Compare current authority. Each person chooses a card privately. Reveal the cards together and explain the differences. Agree who decides today.
4. Agree desired authority. Reveal the cards, discuss differences and specify what each side wants to transfer or accept.
5. Plan the transition. Agree the information, skills, support and boundaries needed. Record the agreement on the Delegation Board. Set a review point using real decisions.

The board records the agreement. People need to sustain it. Check whether the recipient could exercise that power and whether the giver respected the decision.

Before asking for more autonomy, agree which decision one side is willing to release and the other to accept.
