Teams can share a goal and still compete for the same capacity.

**Why it matters:** A product manager or product owner's judgment also shows in what they say no to. Each yes commits capacity and can put another promise at risk.

**Make cooperation worth doing:** A shared goal needs complementary contributions. If helping another team puts a commitment you are judged on at risk, the organization asks you to meet incompatible expectations.

A [research review on cooperation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-021-09777-w) linked this connection between teams' goals with more open discussion of disagreements. Cooperation makes sense when progress toward your goal also helps someone reach theirs.

Many studies measured those relationships at one point in time. The association does not establish that announcing a shared goal improves performance.

In a [study of one software organization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.00236), people described cooperation problems linked to different team metrics and priorities. They used objectives and key results (OKRs), but sharing a framework did not resolve that conflict.

Goodwill and helpful behavior still matter. Strategy should make helping compatible with each team's commitments. A recurring dependency sustained only through goodwill leaves people resolving contradictions the organization should address.

**Manage the combined demand:** At [Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2020/6/tech-migrations-the-spotify-way), several infrastructure teams requested migrations from the same product teams. Requests arrived separately, sometimes with short deadlines. Recipients had to fit all those demands alongside business features.

Spotify created a common map and negotiated priorities with its technical architecture group. Before adding a migration, they estimated cost, identified affected teams and set deadlines. Each migration had a product manager responsible for it.

That process let people discuss the combined effect of requests on the same capacity. The account reports improvements, without a controlled comparison or quantified aggregate result.

For example, imagine Sales needs a data export for a prospective deal. Product wants to improve billing to reduce failed renewals. Both need Platform before month-end, but Platform can support one request.

Each demand makes sense separately. Meeting them requires a decision about the combined demand.

**Compare through a common language:** Without shared criteria, prioritization can become opinion against opinion. Political pressure or the [HiPPO](https://hci.stanford.edu/seminar/abstracts/07-08/080125-kohavi.html), the highest paid person's opinion, can prevail. People may also avoid deciding and do whatever they can manage.

Making prioritization explicit starts with the criteria and assumptions. We could compare the two requests through these dimensions:

- Strategic contribution: which outcome we want to support.
- Expected benefit and evidence: what we expect and why we believe it.
- Cost of delay: what we lose if we wait.
- Capacity and dependencies: whom we need and what other work we would displace.
- Risks and obligations: which limits constrain the choice.

Criteria give people something shared to discuss. They can examine a benefit, an estimate or the cost of waiting without only defending their position.

The dimensions and their importance also express a strategic choice. Estimates retain uncertainty, and weights require judgment. A common language helps people explain the decision and challenge its assumptions.

**Agree what gets capacity:** If the shared intent is retaining existing revenue, Sales and Product compare both requests against those criteria. They could propose the billing change first, explaining its expected benefit and the cost of delaying the export.

The director responsible for that outcome decides, records the trade-off and sets a review date. Sales adjusts its promise. Platform receives one feasible commitment agreed across teams, rather than two incompatible urgent requests.

Managing demand turns separate requests into work we can commit to doing.

In our [work across Adevinta](/work/adevinta/), quarterly alignment helped surface dependencies and coordinate cross-border workstreams. That work depended on leaders, managers, coaches and teams across the group.

For the next contested priority:

1. Agree which shared outcome guides the choice.
2. Gather the demands competing for the same capacity.
3. Compare them through the same dimensions. Make uncertainty visible.
4. Name who decides and which commitments must change.
5. Record what will happen, what will wait and what evidence would justify revisiting the decision.

Alignment needs a shared intent that makes cooperation worthwhile and a choice people can act on.
