Consider a junior developer who brings you a solution built with AI. The automated checks pass. You still believe the task needs someone with more experience.

**Why it matters:** AI may let juniors attempt work that used to require more experience. We need to check which limits still protect the product, and give newcomers ways to develop the judgment those limits require.

## Make the experience requirement specific

In [Hunger beats tenure](https://felixrieseberg.com/hunger-beats-tenure-advice-for-junior-engineers/), Felix Rieseberg describes newcomers using AI to build prototypes and gain influence. He also recounts accepting a limitation that a new colleague later questioned with success.

Experience can help us spot constraints a convincing demonstration leaves out. But some of what we remember may depend on tools or conditions that have changed.

Before deciding that a junior cannot take on a task, I would explain which part requires more experience. Perhaps they need to recognise a risk, understand a dependency or make a product decision. Naming that requirement gives us something to examine together.

I would also ask what evidence would change my view and make room to test that concern with them. A requirement we can explain and test gives the junior a way to progress. An unexplained objection can leave them waiting to become someone we regard as experienced enough.

## Keep juniors involved in the learning

My concern is that a junior can obtain a solution while missing the encounters with a problem through which they develop judgment. AI can make something appear feasible while leaving important conditions unexamined.

In a [randomised experiment with 52 programmers](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v2), Shen and Tamkin asked participants to complete tasks using an unfamiliar Python library. The AI group scored lower on an immediate test of understanding. The researchers found no statistically significant improvement in completion time.

Many participants had years of programming experience. The study measured learning a new library, with no long-term follow-up. We still need evidence about how junior developers acquire expertise over time with AI.

AI may also help juniors develop judgment by giving them more opportunities to test an idea. They need to focus each iteration on what they learn: what they expected, what happened and how the result changes their next decision.

In a review, I would ask what they learned about the solution and its value to users, whether the attempt succeeded or failed. We need to give them time to reflect and apply that learning when deciding what to try next.

## Review value as well as correctness

For a junior presenting an AI-assisted solution, I would ask:

- Why does it work, and which assumptions does it depend on?
- What risks remain, and how have you checked them?
- What should improve for someone using the product?
- What evidence would make you revise the solution or the product idea?

I want to help develop professionals who can reason about creating value through successive changes to a product. That includes deciding whether the next change is worth making.

A junior who can explain those decisions and support them with evidence gives me a reason to reconsider how much experience that task requires.

I would allow more scope where the team has suitable checks and a way to recover. A pipeline of automated checks and the ability to restore an earlier version can support that decision. We still need to assess whether those safeguards cover the consequences of this change.

AI can let a junior produce a much larger change. I would scale review and safeguards to the possible harm. Passing the checks alone does not establish whether the product became more useful.

## Give newcomers a path to judgment

Developing those capabilities also requires opportunities to practise them.

[Anthropic's internal employment study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic) reports a smaller proportion of junior technical hires and greater emphasis on autonomy and judgment in hiring evaluations. This describes one rapidly growing AI company. It does not establish the future of entry-level work across the profession.

An employer raising that expectation also needs to consider how newcomers can develop the judgment it requires.

If we expect juniors to reason about a product, we need to involve them in that reasoning. Experienced colleagues can help them identify overlooked consequences and review what they learn from testing an idea. The junior should be able to explain what improved for users, or why the team decided to try something else.

Those of us with more experience have a responsibility too. I need to listen to the reasoning and accept evidence that challenges my view, including when it comes from the junior.

At the next review, ask the junior to explain the value and risks behind a decision. Then name the evidence that would make you reconsider the experience you believe that task requires.
